# RALF — Research Prospectus

*A working document. Where the work is going, what it argues, who I want to do it with.*

— Brandon Brown · May 2026 · draft v0.1

> Status note: This is a draft. The shape is right. The prose will sharpen. The arguments will get more specific as the work that grounds them gets more specific. Send pushback.

---

Some of the artists and scholars I most respect — Yuval Noah Harari, Wade Davis, John Vervaeke — work primarily in text. Their authority comes from a deep literacy in written traditions, and the rigor of what they do is real. But the things I most want to point at are not written in books. They are written in bodies, in songs, in the way fifty people can coordinate without anyone telling them to.

So a question: what does the equivalent intellectual work look like when your primary text is a roda, a dance floor, a jazz combo, a bombazo?

This document is my working answer. It lays out a stance, an argument, and a plan — for RALF (Responsive Audio Locomotion Framework, the system I have been building for over a year) and for the broader project RALF sits inside.

---

## The stance: practitioner-theorist

There are three positions you could take toward an embodied tradition.

You could observe it from outside. That's ethnography. You can do it carefully and respectfully and still produce work that captures the form's surface — what people do, when they do it, what they say it means — without quite touching what the form is *for* on the inside.

You could practice it without thinking about it. That's most participants in most traditions, and there is nothing wrong with that. The tradition does not need theorists to keep working.

Or you could practice it and think about what you are practicing — let the practice and the thinking inform each other, and translate what you find into language a reader who doesn't share the practice can follow. That's what I am trying to do. Call it practitioner-theorist. Christopher Small worked this way in *Musicking* — he was a cellist as much as he was a writer — and that book is the most direct touchstone for me. The position has been held by others. It's just not the dominant mode of public intellectual work, which is heavily text-bound and tends to flatten what it can't translate.

I want to be specific about what I bring and what I don't.

I bring years of capoeira — enough to know the difference between a game where the players are dialoguing and a game where they are performing. I bring twenty years on piano, more than half of those in groups where you find the music together rather than executing it. I bring years of DJing in Chicago and Detroit lineage rooms where the floor is a co-author. I bring contemplative practice, which has taught me something about the inside of attention. And I bring twenty years of building software for a living, which is the other half of how I think.

I do not bring the kind of training in any single tradition that an elder or a master practitioner brings. I am not standing in for capoeira mestres or bomba *bomberas* or jazz lifers. What I can do is hold these forms next to each other, name patterns that recur, and put those patterns in conversation with ideas from elsewhere — political philosophy, systems theory, cognitive science.

That's the position. The argument follows from it.

## Three patterns: a case-study reading

I want to describe what I see before I name it.

In a capoeira roda: two players in the middle, a circle of clapping around them, the berimbau setting the toque. The game is competitive — you are trying to put your partner in compromised positions, get them to react, control the spatial conversation. But there is no winning. The game is good when both players are reading each other clearly, when the dialogue stays alive, when the berimbau and the chorus and the players are in one breathing thing. If you dominate too completely, the game collapses. If you can't read your partner, the game gets boring. The system rewards mutual attentiveness more than it rewards strength.

In a jazz session: the pianist solos, the bass walks under, the drummer lays a frame. Twenty bars in, the bass hears something — a harmonic implication the piano just threw out — and takes the lead. The pianist hears the shift and moves to comping. Nobody called a meeting. The leadership rotated because the music asked for it. The session runs on continuous readjustment.

On a Friday night dance floor, four hundred bodies in a room. The DJ is reading them. They are reading her. The bodies entrain on the kick, find each other in the hi-hat, lock in around a phrase that gets longer the closer to peak you get. People come in. People leave. The collective has texture, weight, and a forward motion that is not produced by any one person.

Now a particular kind of reading.

I want to treat these forms as practical philosophies of human relating — carriers of insight, expressed in body and sound rather than in text, about how people can cooperate under pressure, metabolize conflict, and keep their systems alive over time. What follows is one example of what that kind of reading yields. Three patterns I find recurring, and what each one suggests.

A note about the status of the list. These are not the three things these forms are about. They are three patterns I find particularly worth pointing at right now, in a body of practice that is much bigger and richer than any short summary. The reading is illustrative — a case study in what taking these forms seriously as practical philosophy reveals. Other patterns are surely there. Other readers, looking at the same forms, would surface different ones. What I'm hoping to show is that the method is generative — that there is real wisdom here, expressed in registers most public intellectual discourse doesn't know how to read.

### One — cooperation over domination, which unleashes creativity

In each form there is a working taboo on domination. It's structural, not just moral. The system does not reward dominators. A capoeira player who only crushes is not a strong player; they are a partner you don't want to game with. A jazz musician who only overpowers is not a strong soloist; they are a colleague who can't listen. A DJ who only forces the room is not a good DJ; they are a sound source.

Because dominators don't win, the energy that would go into dominance gets redirected. It goes into reading your partner more closely. It goes into making your move interesting, because your move has to land inside a conversation rather than on top of one. It goes into the long game of getting better at the form.

The parallel in geopolitics is one Yuval Noah Harari made on a recent podcast and which has been kicking around in my head since. For most of human history, empires invested heavily in military because the international order was zero-sum — weak states got conquered. The post-WWII order put a taboo on conquest. The taboo was imperfect, often violated, sometimes hypocritical. But it shifted incentives. Energy that had gone into militarization was freed for healthcare, science, the welfare state. Harari credits the taboo for much of what we recognize as modern flourishing.

I see the same logic at small scale in these embodied forms. They are not nonviolent because their practitioners are nice. They are nonviolent because the structure of the practice penalizes domination and rewards conversation. Take the taboo off — make the goal *winning by force* — and you don't get a better capoeira. You get a fight. A fight produces a much smaller range of human capacities than the game does.

### Two — conflict gets resolved without a winner

These forms have conflict in them. Capoeira is explicitly built around play-fighting. Rumba is courtship with real edge. Jazz has aesthetic disagreements that play out in real time on the bandstand. The dance floor has moments where a body in your space changes the geometry of your dance.

What the forms do with conflict is the interesting part. They process it. They don't pretend it isn't there, and they don't let it tear the form apart. The game continues. The song keeps playing. The room stays together.

The mechanism is something like: conflict gets channeled into expression rather than into combat. In capoeira your aggression becomes a sequence of movements that puts your partner in a position they have to read and respond to. They respond, and now you have a richer game. In rumba, advance and evasion play out as the *vacunao* and the dance away from it. In jazz, your disagreement with the rhythm section becomes a solo that argues, and the rhythm section argues back. The conflict turns into musical content.

The parallel in liberal political traditions is voting and the conventions of democratic disagreement. When the system is working, disagreement gets routed into ballots, into legislative debate, into court cases. It produces outcomes that aren't consensus but are legitimate enough that everyone agrees to abide by them. The energy of disagreement becomes the engine of the system rather than its disintegration.

Both registers — the bandstand and the legislature — depend on a shared commitment to keep the form going *through* the disagreement. When that commitment breaks, both degrade in similar ways. Combat in the roda. Coup in the country.

### Three — the system self-corrects and adapts

Jazz has two clocks. One is fast: inside a tune, the rhythm section adjusts in real time to whatever the soloist is doing, and the soloist adjusts to the rhythm section. Mistakes get absorbed. The piece holds shape. Drift gets corrected without anyone having to call it out.

The other clock is slow: jazz as a form has evolved over a century. New harmonic vocabularies came in. New rhythmic feels came in. The form absorbed what was useful and discarded what didn't survive playing. Nobody designed the evolution. The form self-corrects at the scale of decades the same way it self-corrects at the scale of measures.

Capoeira does the same thing. A game self-corrects inside itself — a player who is over-extending gets read and exploited, learns, adjusts. And capoeira as a tradition has evolved: regional and angola, contemporary game choices, the conversation between schools. Different communities have made different choices. The tradition has held.

Liberal political traditions are also explicitly self-correcting at two clocks. Inside a moment: free press, independent courts, opposition parties, civil society — the mechanisms that catch power overreaching and bring it back into bounds. Over a longer time: constitutional amendment, generational shifts, the law moving as the country moves.

Both kinds of system — the musical and the political — seem to me to be built around the same recognition: nothing stays correctly tuned on its own. You have to put the correcting capacity inside the system, or the system breaks the first time it encounters real perturbation.

---

Three patterns. There are more. The reason these three landed for me first is the parallel — that versions of the same logic show up at very different scales, in the liberal international order at its best moments, in democratic institutions at their best moments. That parallel is not the point of the case study. It's a corroboration. It is one striking piece of evidence that what these forms encode is worth taking seriously as practical wisdom about how humans can relate, not just as cultural materials to admire or preserve.

More patterns are there to be found. I expect the work over the coming years to continue surfacing them.

## What I am not claiming

A few clarifications before going further, because the argument is easy to misread.

I am not saying these forms *are* liberal democracies, or that liberal institutions are downstream of them. They are practices held inside communities with specific histories and lineages. Many of them predate liberalism by centuries, and most of them arose under conditions of profound un-liberty. I am not arguing for derivation in either direction. The parallel I draw above is one striking analogy — useful as corroboration, useful as a way of saying *look at what's at stake here* — not a comprehensive theory of where good institutions come from.

I am not claiming the three patterns I named are exhaustive, or even the most important. Other readers will draw out different patterns. I expect to draw out more myself as the work continues. The three are this document's case study; they are not the inventory.

I am not romanticizing. These traditions contain everything human traditions contain. Hierarchies, exclusions, internal politics, periods of degradation. The patterns I named are tendencies the forms have when they are working, not guarantees they always live out. Capoeira has had moments where commercialization pulled it toward spectacle. Jazz has had its own contests with the recording industry, the academy, money. The forms can be diminished. The wisdom they carry survives the diminishment, but it is not automatic.

I am not extracting design principles for general use. There is a long, ugly tradition of taking embodied practices — especially Black and Indigenous embodied practices — abstracting their patterns, and reselling those patterns as productivity techniques or management frameworks. That move strips the ethics, the lineage, the accountability. I want no part of it. The work here points at the patterns precisely so the ethics can travel with them, not so the patterns can be lifted clean.

What I am claiming, narrowly, is this: these forms can be read as practical philosophies of human relating, and the reading is worth doing. There is real wisdom here, and it does not all reduce to text. The three patterns above are one demonstration of what such a reading yields. The political-scale parallel is one demonstration that the wisdom isn't parochial. And if you want to build new systems — performance systems, institutional systems, technological systems — that operate by what these forms know, the forms are a living body of working examples to study, practice inside, and build alongside.

## RALF: the system as argument

This is where the prospectus turns from the intellectual frame to the work.

RALF creates real-time, mutually transformative dialogue between movement and music. A dancer moves. The system reads the movement as continuous qualities — velocity, jerkiness, coherence between limbs, spatial extent, contraction. Those qualities feed a compositional layer that triggers and shapes music. The music in turn shapes what the dancer does next. The loop closes.

Two things to notice about the architecture, because they bear on the philosophical argument.

First, the system does not let either side dominate. The dancer cannot directly trigger sounds the way a button triggers a sample. The system reads movement qualities and *composes a response*, weighted by chance and shaped by the configured intent for the scene. The music is not playback. It is generated in real time in response to the body. Either party can break the loop. The loop is interesting because both parties are continuously adjusting.

Second, the system is built around a vocabulary of intents and constraints, not around a tight one-to-one mapping. There is a constraint grammar — the kinds of things this scene is asking the dancer to do, the kinds of musical materials available to the system — and inside that grammar, improvisation is welcomed. This is the same shape the embodied forms have. The clave is fixed; what you play against it is open. The toque is set; what you do inside it is yours.

So the system, when it is working, is doing in microcosm what the three patterns from the case study name. Cooperation over domination — the loop only works if neither party tries to control. Conflict that produces content — when the system surprises the dancer or the dancer surprises the system, the surprise becomes the next move. Self-correction — the loop continuously adjusts; nothing has to come back to a fixed score.

I want to be honest about what this means. The architecture is shaped by what I have been reading in these forms. If I were foregrounding different patterns from the same body of practice, the architecture would likely take a different shape. RALF is one possible system you could build by taking these forms seriously as practical philosophy. The point of building it is not that the three patterns are the right three. The point is that the practice of reading these forms produces working insight — and that insight can build things.

This is what I mean when I say the system itself is the argument. You don't have to follow my lateral thinking. You can dance in it and feel something. What you feel is one version of what these forms know.

## Three tiers of work

I see three concentric kinds of work RALF makes possible. They support each other.

### Tier 1 — Documentation and analysis with practitioners in established traditions

Capoeira mestres and contemporary players. Bomba *bomberas* and *primo* drummers. House dancers and DJs. Rumberos. West African drummers. Practitioners who are already thinking deeply about what their traditions are doing.

I bring RALF as both a creative tool and a documentation instrument. We collaborate on pieces or sessions that lean into specific features of their tradition. The system makes visible what is usually tacit — coordination patterns, energy curves, transitions, repair moments, the way a piquete lands on a primo response. We produce performances and we produce analysis. The practitioners shape the work as much as I do. They are co-authors, not subjects.

The point of this tier is two-fold. It deepens the intellectual project — these are the people who can correct my readings and tell me what I am missing. And it produces real artistic work in lineage with these traditions.

### Tier 2 — Experimental collaborations across traditions

Cross-tradition work that wouldn't happen without something like RALF as connective tissue. A capoeirista and a house dancer in conversation with the system mediating the relationship between their movement and a shared evolving score. A jazz musician composing for a bomba dancer who has never seen the music in advance. A choreographer and a producer working with the system as a third collaborator.

The point of this tier is to take the structural commitments named above — cooperation, conflict-as-content, self-correction — and run them in contexts where they don't already have a centuries-old infrastructure. Where they have to be reinvented in real time, with the participants and the system co-figuring how the container holds.

This tier is also where most of the formally innovative work will happen. New compositional ideas. New ways of staging. New audiences. The risk and the upside are both larger here.

### Tier 3 — The North Star: pulling a Western institution toward improvisation

The aspirational target is something like this: Alonzo King LINES Ballet — or a company of similar caliber — commissions a piece. A composer with the range of someone like Jason Moran writes it. The piece is structured for RALF. The dancers are not executing fixed choreography against a fixed score. They are inside a system where their movement is shaping the music in real time, and the music is shaping their movement, inside the constraint grammar the composer has set.

If it worked, what it would demonstrate is that the structural logic of Afro-diasporic musicking can be brought into the most formally trained Western performance institutions and produce something new there — not by replacing what those institutions know, but by adding a dimension they have largely set aside.

Alonzo King is the right kind of target because LINES already lives at the intersection of Western ballet training and other musical and movement vocabularies. King has been bringing world musicians and non-ballet sensibilities into ballet for decades. RALF would extend that move into the structural commitments themselves. Not just the materials, but the way music and movement co-produce each other.

This is a stretch goal. It is also concrete enough to orient by.

## Open questions I am sitting with

The honest list of things I do not yet know.

- **What form does the first piece take?** A documented session with one or two practitioners? A small public showing? A short film? A live performance with a single dancer? Each shape has different setup costs and different downstream usefulness, and the answer probably emerges from the first conversations with collaborators rather than from sitting alone with the question.
- **Which practitioners do I want as the first Tier-1 collaborators?** People whose traditions and intellectual temperament make them generative partners — who would find RALF interesting rather than threatening.
- **What's the right shape for the writing alongside the work?** The book *Technologies of Remembering* is one track. Standalone essays are another. Documentation of practice (notes, recordings, transcripts of sessions with collaborators) is a third. Possibly all three, with different audiences, evolving as the practice does.
- **What does institutional support look like for this kind of work, and where does it already exist?** Bay Area dance presenters, generative-media programs (Gray Area, ZERO1), university-affiliated residencies, music institutions open to this frame. The mapping is more open than I would have guessed.
- **Where do I draw the line on Tier 2 work that risks abstracting the patterns away from their lineages?** This is the ongoing ethical edge. The forms have ethics. The tools should not strip them.

## What this document is for

A few audiences will see this prospectus.

Funders and program officers — to understand that RALF is part of a larger intellectual project, not a discrete tech-art experiment.

Potential collaborators — to see whether the frame resonates with what they're already doing, and where the conversation might be.

Institutional partners, eventually — to see that the artist they would be working with has a coherent body of thinking, not just a coherent body of code.

And, honestly, me. I needed to write this so I could see the shape of the work in one piece. Things keep coming clearer the more I have to articulate them. This is draft v0.1. Future versions will be sharper.
