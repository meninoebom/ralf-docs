## Conversation 1 — "What kind of public intellectual?"

**Source:** https://claude.ai/share/cf92150b-3c6d-479f-87f1-80d1997137ff

### Brandon's opening

> When I think about some of the people that I respect and I sort of want to emulate — Yuval Noah Harari, Wade Davis, John Vervaeke, the cognitive scientist from Canada — their lingua franca, the waters they swim in, are books, are texts. And I'm interested in things that, at least the way I propose them or the way I view them, are written in people's bodies and in their music. What would the equivalent work be? What would it look like? How could I represent as a public intellectual the reading of music and movement — of *musicking*, to use Chris Small's term — as a text through which we can see human cooperation, the sublimation of human conflict, the engine of human progress?

### Brandon naming what these traditions argue

> When you look at the characteristics that are shared across a lot of different musicking traditions, I'm really interested in the ones in the African American tradition — and I mean that in the broad sense of American throughout the Caribbean and South America. In those traditions, you will find there are these coordination protocols where there is a shared cycle that creates a kind of cadence, a language. There's a shared movement language, a shared rhythmic language, maybe shared melodic material. And within that is improvisatory skill being developed. So you're developing the human being, but you're also developing the connection with the community. You're developing timing and coordination. You're developing what Chris Small would say is a picture, or a sort of argument for, or a metaphor for what ideal human relations look like. And you're saying they look like democracy. They can sublimate conflict. They can create cohesion. They can support unique contributions and expressions for multiple people, and they get enriched by diversity and by the input of every single participant rather than needing to be owned or hierarchically managed from a small group.

### Claude's reflection — the practitioner-theorist stance

> You're not trying to *become* a textual scholar analyzing from the outside. You're proposing yourself as a practitioner-theorist who reads these traditions *from within* their own logic and epistemology. You're saying: here's what democracy looks like when you *feel* it in your body moving with fifty other people in a roda, or when you're listening for the drum call and responding in real time. That's a different kind of rigor entirely.
>
> The work would be: articulating that argument in public-facing ways — through writing, yes, but also through documentation of practice, through teaching, through creating frameworks that make visible what's usually tacit. You're doing what Harari does with history or Davis does with ethnography, except your *primary text* is the lived coordination protocol itself, and you're translating it into language that lets people *see* the political philosophy embedded in it.

### Brandon's three-tier vision for RALF

> I think both are the things I want to do. I want to work with people from established traditions, and I want to work in a kind of context in which they can accentuate certain aspects of the tradition and play with them and explore them in a way that accentuates them for the research work — and as performance. And then on the other side of that, I do want to work in an innovative way.
>
> My gold standard, the North Star for me, is to build a system robust enough that Alonzo King LINES Ballet could work with the system and work with a score and get somebody like Jason Moran, or somebody even maybe more a little bit futuristic, to compose a piece that runs through the RALF system and the dancers get to affect it. So we're taking the African American, broadly speaking, paradigm or approach and applying it to a more Western performative space — the hybrid performing space where Alonzo brings that tradition into ballet — and we kind of pull it more toward an African tradition where there is improvisation and there's a collaboration with the music. That's a North Star on the innovative side.
>
> And then on the side of working in tradition, I would love to create situations where capoeiristas and bomberas and rumbistas and house dancers — whoever — come in and work with them, bring music into the system, create a collaborative project, and make work.

### Claude's mirroring of the three tiers

> You've got three tiers: documentation and analysis work with practitioners in established traditions, experimental collaborations *within* those traditions using RALF as a creative tool, and then this aspirational North Star — using RALF to pull a Western ballet institution like Alonzo King LINES toward a more participatory, improvisatory, African diaspora-influenced model of creation where the dancers and composer and system are all in real-time dialogue.

### Brandon's timeline

> Not this year. For the Alonzo King, I'm going to give myself four or five years. But by the end of this year, I want to be an artist. I want to be doing work. And then I want to be gaining institutional acceptance or help or support in the next year. And between those, in those five years or four years between that, I want to build a reputation and great work and great technology until I'm in a position to work with a team like the Alonzo King folks.

### Claude's closing suggestion — the practitioner-theorist thread

> As you're building out that document, keep a thread running through it that connects your own practice — capoeira, piano, your contemplative work — to the research vision. Because the strongest version of this isn't you studying these traditions from outside. It's you as a practitioner-theorist whose *own* embodied knowledge is part of the apparatus. That's what makes it different from ethnography.

### Open question Brandon left on the table

> What does "being an artist and doing work" look like by December? Are we talking a premiere performance, a documented collaborative series, a publication, all of the above? — *I have no idea. I've got to do more thinking about that.*

## Conversation 2 — "Three dimensions: musicking as political philosophy"

**Source:** https://claude.ai/share/05b70612-1b0a-4382-82ab-bf871a61938d

### Brandon's framing — the Harari parallel

> I just heard from Yuval Noah Harari on the Ezra Klein podcast today. One of the points he makes is that for most of human history, empires have had to invest more in military than anything else because there was a race to become the strongest. That was the incentive, because there was a zero-sum paradigm — weak nations couldn't exist; they could just be wiped out. Every nation had an incentive to put a huge amount of energy into weapons and defense.
>
> Now fast forward to the post-WWII international order — it put a taboo on invading countries in opposition to their sovereignty. And what it did is free up energy to be invested in healthcare, creativity, innovation, etc.
>
> On a very microcosmic level, this is what you see in certain Afro-diasporic musicking practices. In capoeira, you sublimate conflict. The games are better because it is not the domination of the strong that makes for a good game or keeps this infinite-game practice going. It is the involvement of everyone in a taboo on domination in favor of the promotion of what I call mutually transformative exchange and engagement. You dial your game in such a way to create dialogue.
>
> You find this throughout a lot of different kinds of musicking practices where it's not so much about domination as it is about communication, conversation, diversity. What happens is you free up energy from the energy that would be poured into domination. On the level of the thing itself, having capoeira exist may sublimate certain kinds of conflict and free up energy for creativity. But also within the form, there may be these rules that incline the practice toward cooperation, conversation, away from domination and award-giving and winning and authorship. This is where I see a kind of pointer toward a political and social philosophy living in these practices.

### Claude's reflection

> Capoeira operates on an analogous logic — there's a taboo on domination, which means the energy that *would* go into crushing your opponent instead goes into dialogue, mutuality, keeping the game alive as an infinite practice rather than a finite contest. And then you're seeing this pattern ripple outward across Afro-diasporic musicking more broadly: the forms themselves encode a social philosophy where the rules actually incentivize cooperation and diversity over winningness. It's not just that capoeira *happens* to be nonviolent — it's that the *structure* of the practice continuously pulls practitioners toward communication and away from ego-driven authorship.
>
> You're pointing at something deeper than just "these practices are nice and cooperative" — you're saying they're *laboratories* for alternative economic and political logics. They're living proofs that you can design systems where flourishing is collective, where constraints generate creativity rather than scarcity.
>
> That's a really different frame for positioning your work with RALF, actually. This isn't just about choreomusical technology — it's about encoding values at the system level.

### Brandon — why start with RALF rather than text

> I want to start with RALF, because then it won't just be words and people won't have to struggle to follow my lateral thinking. I'm a very lateral thinker. RALF gives me a way to ground these things. But I want RALF to be a platform from which I can make these arguments and draw these parallels.

### Brandon — the three dimensions

> There are three dimensions where I see the musicking practices I'm interested in encoding and pointing at some patterns we see in the liberal international order. It's not so much specific to liberalism, but to any kind of certain relationship between people that we can find happening throughout history. Liberalism is one version of it.
>
> **Dimension one: Cooperation over domination.** By prioritizing cooperation over domination, there's a freeing up of energy that would be used for protection and dominance, and that energy gets unleashed into creativity and innovation.
>
> **Dimension two: Conflict resolution.** Conflict gets sublimated and resolved by allowing different parties to express themselves and coming up with mechanisms where you can reconcile differences and allow for difference. You see that in these art forms in the way that some games are competitive but without a winner — there's always a way that everybody's input is honored. You see that in liberal society in the form of voting.
>
> **Dimension three: Self-correction and adaptation.** The way that complex systems tend to do. You see this in musicking systems where there are mechanisms both on the system level for adaptation and evolution — jazz evolves as an art form. But within the jazz idiom, there are self-correcting mechanisms within a song that allow a group to adapt to each other and move and be in the moment, creatively adapting to changing context. You see that on a level of politics in the form of the media, the state — meant to help correct the ills and wrongs of unbridled power. You see that in the checks and balances that come from post-Enlightenment constitutional approaches to politics. Those checks and balances are meant to create self-correction.
>
> All three of these are dimensions on which I see a sort of politics encoded — a politics and a philosophy of social relations — encoded in musicking traditions that I'm interested in.

### Claude's synthesis

> Three interlocking dimensions: cooperation over domination, which unlocks creativity and innovation; conflict resolution mechanisms that honor everyone's voice without requiring a winner; and self-correction systems that allow the whole thing to adapt and evolve in real time.
>
> And you're saying these aren't just nice features of Afro-diasporic musicking — they're actually blueprints for how functional political systems work. Capoeira's dialogue, jazz's collective adaptation, the way these forms have survived and evolved through centuries of constraint and creativity — that's not separate from liberalism's checks and balances or voting or the press. It's the same underlying logic, just embodied differently.
>
> What makes this powerful for RALF is that you're not arguing for this abstractly. You're building a system where gesture recognition, real-time synthesis, and the feedback loop between dancer and sound *has* to operate on these principles to work. The system itself becomes an argument. You can't dominate the system — you have to dialogue with it. It self-corrects. It adapts. It creates space for everyone's input.
>
> That's the frame that takes RALF from "cool choreomusical tool" to "this is what a different kind of politics looks like in practice."
